### The Profitability Problem Most Shops Ignore

Here's a scenario every garage owner knows: You finish a brake job, charge $450, and feel good about the margin. But did you actually make money?

Most shop owners quote jobs based on flat-rate labor guides and estimated parts costs. The problem? Real-world labor time rarely matches the book, and parts markup varies wildly between suppliers.

Without tracking actual costs, you're flying blind.

### What "True" Job Profitability Means

True job profitability isn't just revenue minus parts cost. It's:

Revenue - (Parts Cost + Actual Labor Cost + Supplies) = Real Profit

Where:
- Actual Labor Cost = Hours worked × Technician's hourly cost to you (not what you charge the customer)
- Supplies = Shop materials, fluids, consumables

If your tech took 3 hours on a 2-hour book job, and you're paying them $35/hour, that's an extra $35 eating into your margin.

### The Hidden Costs That Kill Margins

#### 1. Technician Efficiency Variance

A technician billing at 80% efficiency means 20% of their clocked time isn't generating revenue. If you're paying $35/hour for 8 hours, that's $56/day—or over $14,000/year—in lost productivity.

#### 2. Parts Markup Inconsistency

If your team quotes parts without checking current supplier prices, you might be working on outdated margins. A $20 swing on a part can turn a profitable job into a break-even job.

#### 3. Comebacks and Warranty Work

Unpaid labor on comebacks directly hurts profitability. If you're not tracking which services generate comebacks, you can't fix the problem.

### How to Start Tracking Job Profitability

#### Step 1: Know Your True Labor Cost

Calculate what each technician actually costs you:
- Hourly wage
- Benefits and taxes (typically 20-30% on top of wage)
- Training time

This is your loaded labor cost—the real number that matters.

#### Step 2: Track Actual Hours Per Job

Stop estimating. Use a time clock or job management system that logs when a tech starts and stops each job. Compare actual hours to book hours.

#### Step 3: Calculate Margin on Every Job

For each completed job:
1. Total revenue
2. Subtract parts cost (what you paid, not what you charged)
3. Subtract (actual hours × loaded labor cost)
4. Subtract supplies/consumables

What's left is your real profit.

### Red Flags That Signal Profit Leakage

- Jobs consistently taking longer than quoted
- Certain service categories with thin margins (often oil changes)
- High-efficiency techs on low-margin jobs (misallocation)
- Parts costs higher than expected on specific repairs

### The Payoff of Tracking Profitability

Shops that track true job profitability often find:

- 15-20% of jobs are actually unprofitable
- Certain service types need price adjustments
- Specific technicians consistently over or under book time
- Parts suppliers with better margins for certain categories

This data lets you make informed decisions instead of guessing.

### Start With One Week of Data

You don't need fancy software to start. For one week, track every job:
- What you charged
- What you paid for parts
- How long the tech actually worked
- Your loaded labor cost per hour

The results will likely surprise you.

### Take Control of Your Margins

Profitability isn't about working harder—it's about working smarter. When you know which jobs make money and which don't, you can:

- Adjust pricing on low-margin services
- Route profitable jobs to your most efficient techs
- Negotiate better parts pricing
- Stop accepting work that costs you money

Your shop's profitability is hiding in plain sight. You just need to look at the numbers.
